Never split the difference. Get to "that's right."

Chris Voss's system, distilled to eighteen cards and pointed at the conversations we actually have: listing appointments, price reductions, buyer-agent calls, vendors. Same card shape as the Content Machine Hub. Read the thesis, steal the tactics, use the last line today.

Compromise is how both sides lose. The move is not meeting in the middle, it is understanding the other side so precisely that they hand you the middle.

Bank One

Make them feel heard before you ask.

The whole system rests on one move: make the other side feel heard before you ask them for anything. Not agreement. Recognition. Everything in Banks 2 and 3 fails without this.

Tactical Empathy6 cards
Tactical Empathy
Empathy here is not sympathy and it is not agreement. It is the deliberate act of naming the other side's perspective and feelings out loud so accurately that they relax. You are not conceding anything by understanding them. You are getting the information nobody hands over to a person they are guarding against.
  • Separate the person from the problem, then go hunting for what is driving the person.
  • Aim at the unspoken feeling under the stated position. The position is rarely the real thing.
  • Say what you hear before you say what you want. Order matters more than wording.
  • Never assume you know. Test the read out loud and let them correct you.
  • Your goal in the first five minutes is information, not persuasion.
Ch. 2, "Be a Mirror"
In our worldA seller who says "I need $400,000" is almost never talking about $400,000. They are talking about the amount they told their brother-in-law, or the number that gets them into the next house. Nothing about that conversation moves until they hear you say the real thing back to them.
The Three Voices
Tone does more work than content. Voss runs three: the positive and playful voice as the default, the late-night FM DJ voice for anchoring a hard point, and the direct or assertive voice as a rare tool that usually costs more than it earns. Your voice is a lever you can pull consciously.
  • Default to positive and playful. A relaxed voice makes the other person's mind relax, and relaxed minds solve problems.
  • Drop into the late-night FM DJ voice, downward inflection, calm and slow, when you deliver a fact you are not negotiating.
  • Use the direct voice almost never. It gets compliance in the room and resentment afterward.
  • Smile before you speak on the phone. It is audible.
  • Inflect up on a question, down on a statement. Never say a fact with a question's tone.
Ch. 2, "Be a Mirror"
In our worldPrice reduction conversations are DJ-voice conversations. The number is calm, slow, and downward. The rest of the call is playful.
Mirroring
Repeat the last one to three words the other person said, with an upward inflection, then be quiet. It is the closest thing to a magic trick in the book. Mirroring signals "help me understand," triggers the other side to elaborate, and buys you thinking time while they do your work for you.
  • Mirror the last three words, or the critical one to three words. Nothing more.
  • Inflect up so it lands as a question, not a parrot.
  • Then shut up. Let the silence do the asking. Four seconds minimum.
  • Use it when someone gives you an answer that is too short or too smooth.
  • Stack it: mirror, silence, they elaborate, mirror the new thing.
Ch. 2, "Be a Mirror"
In our world"We just want to see what the market says." "The market says?" Silence. Now you find out whether they are testing a number or testing you.
Labeling
Name the emotion you detect and give it back to them in a neutral frame: "It seems like...", "It sounds like...", "It looks like...". Labeling a negative defuses it. Labeling a positive reinforces it. Naming the fear out loud shrinks it, because the brain calms once the thing is identified.
  • Start with "it seems like," never "I'm hearing that." Drop the "I." The moment you say I, they brace.
  • Label the negative first and hardest. Fear named is fear reduced.
  • After the label, silence. Do not soften it, do not explain it, do not stack a question on it.
  • Wrong labels are fine and often better. They correct you, and the correction is the information.
  • Label the thing they have not said yet but obviously feel.
Ch. 3, "Don't Feel Their Pain, Label It"
In our world"It seems like you have been burned by an agent before." Then nothing. Half the listing appointments in Richmond turn on that one sentence.
The Accusation Audit
List every bad thing the other side could say about you, and say it first, before they do. Going in with the worst accusations already on the table takes all the air out of them. It sounds like it should hurt you. It does the opposite, because it proves you see the situation clearly and are not hiding.
  • Write the list before the meeting. Every accusation, including the unfair ones.
  • Overstate slightly. "You are probably going to think this is the most arrogant thing you have heard all week."
  • Deliver it early, before you make any ask.
  • Do not defend after. State it, pause, move.
  • The harsher the honest audit, the more credibility you get for everything after it.
Ch. 3, "Don't Feel Their Pain, Label It"
In our worldOpening a price reduction call with "I am about to tell you your house is worth less than I told you it was worth, and you have every right to be furious with me" ends the fight before it starts.
Dynamic Silence
Silence is the tool that makes every other tool work. After a label, a mirror, or a calibrated question, the person who speaks first gives up information. Most people cannot tolerate four seconds of quiet and will fill it with something true.
  • Count four seconds after every label and every mirror. Actually count.
  • Do not soften silence with "does that make sense?" That sentence is a gift you hand back.
  • On the phone, silence feels twice as long. Trust it anyway.
  • Let them fill the space even when you already know the answer. The way they fill it is the data.
  • If the silence gets awkward, that is the tool working.

Bank Two

Own the frame without taking it.

Once they feel heard, the fight becomes about who owns the frame. These are the moves that put you in control without the other side ever feeling controlled.

Control the Frame7 cards
No Is the Start, Not the End
Chasing yes puts people on guard, because yes is a commitment and everybody has been burned by one. No makes people feel safe and in control, and safety is what opens the actual conversation. Invite no on purpose and the real negotiation begins.
  • Ask questions that make no the comfortable answer: "Is now a bad time?" "Have you given up on selling?"
  • Mislabel an emotion deliberately so they say "no, it is not that, it is this." The correction is the truth.
  • Let them say no early. It costs nothing and buys you the whole rest of the call.
  • Never take the first no as final. It usually means "not yet" or "I am not comfortable."
  • Protect their right to say no out loud. It makes them likelier to say yes later.
Ch. 4, "Beware 'Yes'"
In our world"Would it be ridiculous to look at this again in thirty days?" gets an answer. "Can we revisit this in thirty days?" gets a dodge.
The Three Yeses
There are three kinds of yes: counterfeit, confirmation, and commitment. Counterfeit yes is the one where they plan to say no and want you off the phone. Most closes people celebrate are counterfeit. Only commitment yes moves anything, and it always follows a "that's right."
  • Assume any easy yes is counterfeit until it survives a test.
  • Test it with a calibrated question that requires them to do something: "How will you get that approved?"
  • Confirmation yes is a reflex answer to a simple factual question. It means nothing.
  • Commitment yes comes with a plan attached. If there is no plan, there is no commitment.
  • Stop hunting yes. Hunt "that's right."
Ch. 4, "Beware 'Yes'"
In our world"Sounds great, send me the paperwork" with no discussion of timing is counterfeit almost every time. Ask how they want to handle the tenant, and you find out immediately.
"That's Right" Beats "You're Right"
"You're right" means "please stop talking, I am not moving." "That's right" means you have summarized their own position back to them so accurately that they recognize themselves in it, and that recognition is the moment they become willing to move. You get there with a summary, not an argument.
  • Build the summary: label plus paraphrase of their world, in their words, with nothing of yours added.
  • Deliver it, then stop. You are waiting for two specific words.
  • If you get "you're right," you have lost the thread. Go back to labeling.
  • Never say "I understand." It is empty and they know it.
  • The summary must include the thing they have not said out loud.
Ch. 5, "Trigger the Two Words That Immediately Transform Any Negotiation"
In our worldThe Lawrence relocation call. There was a summary available in that conversation about what moving a family to Savannah actually costs a person, and it never got said. That was the miss, and it was not a pricing miss.
Bend Their Reality
Compromise is where both sides lose. Splitting the difference is how you end up with one black shoe and one brown shoe. Instead, work on how the other side sees the deal: what they stand to lose, what the deadline actually does, and what number they anchor to. Fairness, deadlines, and loss aversion are the three levers.
  • Never split the difference. A bad deal is worse than no deal.
  • Use the F word early and once: "I want you to feel fairly treated at every step. Tell me the moment you don't."
  • Anchor emotion first with the worst case, then let the real number feel like relief.
  • Frame around loss, not gain. People work harder to avoid losing than to win.
  • Deadlines are usually softer than they look, including yours. Never reveal you have one.
  • Anchor with a range when you can. "Firms like ours usually see between X and Y."
Ch. 6, "Forge Reality"
In our world"If we list at your number, here is what it costs you in ninety days" beats "here is what you could get if we price it right." Same math, different lever.
Calibrated Questions
Open-ended "how" and "what" questions hand the other side the illusion that they are steering, while you set the direction. They cannot be answered yes or no, they force the other side to solve your problem, and they let you say no without ever saying the word.
  • Start every one with "how" or "what." Cut "why" entirely. Why sounds like an accusation in every language.
  • "How am I supposed to do that?" is the workhorse. It is a no that makes them find your solution.
  • "What about this is important to you?" "What is the biggest challenge you face?"
  • Ask, then shut up. The question is only half the tool.
  • Prepare three to five before any real conversation. You will not invent them under pressure.
Ch. 7, "Create the Illusion of Control"
In our worldA buyer's agent asks for a $12,000 concession. "How am I supposed to take that back to my seller?" They will start building your argument for you.
The Illusion of Control
People defend what they helped build. If the other side designs part of the solution, they are invested in it and will fight for it, sometimes harder than you would. Your job is to steer the design, not to hand over a finished blueprint.
  • Let them name the next step. "What do you think we should do here?"
  • Give them a choice between two things you want, not a yes or no on one thing.
  • Ask them to explain the obstacle, then ask how they would get around it.
  • Never announce your solution before asking for theirs.
  • When they propose something close enough, take it and give them the credit.
Ch. 7, "Create the Illusion of Control"
In our world"What would need to be true for you to be comfortable at that number?" gets you a real path. Presenting a pre-built path gets you a counter.
Say No Without Saying No
There are four ways to decline that keep the relationship and the deal alive. All of them refuse without the word. Aggression triggers aggression, so the skill is holding your line while the other side stays comfortable enough to keep working.
  • "How am I supposed to do that?" Said with respect, it is a refusal and a request for help.
  • "Your offer is very generous, I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me."
  • "I'm sorry, but I'm afraid I just can't do that."
  • "I'm sorry, no." Then the DJ voice and silence.
  • Escalate through them in order. Most negotiations end before the fourth.

Bank Three

Move the number, read the person.

The endgame. How to move a number, how to spot the one fact that reorganizes the whole deal, and how to tell what kind of person you are actually across from.

Close and Read the Room5 cards
The Ackerman Model
A systematic haggling method built on decreasing increments and empathy. Set your target, open at 65% of it, then move to 85, 95, and 100, using calibrated questions and non-round numbers along the way. The shrinking steps convince the other side they have squeezed you to your limit.
  • Set your target price first, and be honest with yourself about it.
  • Open at 65% of target. Use the DJ voice.
  • Calculate three raises in decreasing increments: 85%, 95%, 100%.
  • Use empathy and every form of no before you raise. Make them work for each move.
  • Final number is precise and odd, never round. $37,893 reads as calculated. $38,000 reads as arbitrary.
  • On the final number, throw in a non-monetary item to show you are at your limit.
Ch. 9, "Bargain Hard"
In our worldRepair credits, closing cost negotiations, and vendor contracts. The odd number and the throw-in are the two pieces everyone skips.
Black Swans and the Three Leverages
Every negotiation contains three to five pieces of information that would change everything if you knew them, and the other side does not know they are hiding them. Finding one flips the deal. Leverage comes in three types: positive (what you can give), negative (what you can take away), and normative (their own stated values used to hold them).
  • Assume the unknowns exist. Go looking rather than waiting.
  • The unguarded moments carry them: before the meeting starts, after it ends, in the parking lot.
  • Listen for anything that seems irrational to you. Irrational usually means you are missing a fact.
  • Positive leverage: know what they want. Negative: know what they fear losing. Normative: quote their own principles back.
  • Meet in person when the stakes are high. Black swans do not travel over email.
Ch. 10, "Find the Black Swan"
In our worldThe seller who will not budge on price because of one number is almost always carrying a second number nobody asked about, a payoff, a divorce split, a promise to a parent.
7-38-55 and the Rule of Three
Only 7% of a message is words, 38% is tone, and 55% is body language. When the words and the delivery disagree, believe the delivery. And get every important agreement said three different ways, because a commitment stated once is not a commitment.
  • When words and tone conflict, name the conflict with a label: "It sounds like you agree, but something about this still bothers you."
  • Rule of three: get agreement three times in the same conversation, using different forms.
  • One: the commitment. Two: a label or summary that makes them restate it. Three: a calibrated "how" question that makes them describe execution.
  • Watch for the Pinocchio effect. Liars use more words and more third-person pronouns.
  • Someone saying "I" a lot is not the decision maker. "We" often is.
Ch. 8, "Guarantee Execution"
In our worldA seller agreeing to a staging budget once will forget by Thursday. Three times, one of them being "how do you want to handle the deposit," and it holds.
The Three Bargaining Types
Analyst, Accommodator, Assertive. Each type reads the same behavior differently, and the mistake everyone makes is negotiating with everyone the way they themselves want to be negotiated with. Identify the type in the first few minutes and change your approach, not your position.
  • Analyst: methodical, quiet, silence means thinking. Give data and time. Do not fill their pauses.
  • Accommodator: friendly, talkative, wants the relationship. Warm but often has not checked with the decision maker. Confirm execution.
  • Assertive: direct, wants to be heard above all. Mirror and label until they feel heard. They do not move until they do.
  • Silence means three different things by type. Read it correctly or you will misplay the moment.
  • You are probably one type and default to it. Know which.
Ch. 9, "Bargain Hard"
In our worldAn assertive seller who feels interrupted will not hear a single number you say. The whole call has to be labels until they stop pushing.
Getting to the Table
The most useful email in the book is one line long and works on people who have gone silent, because it invites a no in a way that is almost impossible to ignore. Non-response is not rejection. It is usually discomfort with saying no directly.
  • The email: "Have you given up on this project?" Nothing else in the body.
  • It works because answering no is easier than answering yes, and no restarts the conversation.
  • Use it after two normal follow-ups have gone unanswered, not before.
  • Do not soften it, apologize inside it, or add a paragraph. The one line is the tool.
  • The same shape works out loud: "Have you given up on selling this year?"
