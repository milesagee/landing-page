The Angle

Shape is what the footage is. Angle is who is talking.

The shape is decided by the footage and the cargo is decided by the argument. Neither of them says who is speaking, to whom, or what that person is allowed to claim. That is the angle, it is chosen before pass 1, and until 2026-09-08 this board never asked for it. Every video on this channel had one anyway. Nobody had written it down, so it moved between videos without anyone deciding it should.

The rule this produces

The title, the thumbnail and the first thirty seconds sell the place. The cargo lands inside. Both jobs, one cut, and they do not compete for the same seconds. Selling the place is how the video gets watched. The cargo is how the watcher turns into the right buyer. A video that leads with the cargo gets 76 views and qualifies nobody, because qualifying nobody is what happens when nobody presses play.

The standing angle for this channel

A Richmond local showing you the city, who happens to know what it costs. In that order, always. Not a realtor doing a market update over B-roll, and not a tour guide who never mentions money. The knowing-what-it-costs is what separates it from every other Richmond vlog, and it earns its place by arriving second.

The test, run it before pass 1

Say the angle out loud in one sentence: who is talking, to whom, and what they are allowed to claim. If the sentence is really a description of the topic, there is no angle yet and pass 1 has not started.

GateThe fair housing gate binds the angle before it binds anything else, because the angle is what decides what the video is permitted to say about a neighborhood. The DO NOT CHASE lane above is 27 real queries with real demand behind them, and it is the fastest way to characterize a place by the people in it. It is surfaced here to be recognized and declined. It is never a title, a thumbnail, or an angle.
The Build Order

Nothing is shot in order. Nothing is cut in order either.

There are two lists on every long-form and they are not the same list. Watch order is what the viewer gets. Assembly order is how you get there. Confusing the two is the single reason a long-form stalls, because it makes every missing piece feel like a shoot day instead of a gap.

1 Find the spine
Two things on the timeline. Nothing else.

One question the whole video holds, and the one moment that pays it. Both are almost always already in the footage. Lay those two down and stop. If you cannot say the question in one sentence, the video does not have a spine yet, and you send it back rather than cutting around it.

Not thisStarting at 0:00 and working forward. That is watch order, and it is the last pass, not the first.
2 Cut the blocks, blind
Every block cut to its best 20 to 40 seconds, in no order.

Group every clip by location and light, never by story position. A block is a place at an hour. Cut each one down to its strongest stretch without deciding where it goes in the video. Label them A, B, C. The letters in every brief on this board are these blocks.

Not thisDeciding a block is the opener while you are cutting it. You cannot know that yet and the decision will cost you the clip.
3 Order the blocks
Chapters. This is the pass where they are born.

Now arrange the blocks by what question each one opens and what it closes. Story position gets decided here, on the timeline, not in the camera and not in the folder names. A block only becomes a chapter in this pass, and only if it carries something.

Not thisKeeping the folder order. The Drive is sorted by timestamp, which is the order it happened, which is almost never the order it plays.
4 Name the holes
A short list of pickups with runtimes attached.

Whatever is left is what Miles owes: the intro, the pickups, the numbers. Drop a labeled gap on the timeline with the exact runtime you need and the exact sentence it has to carry. He shoots against a cut that already exists. That is why the intro is shot last, every time.

Not thisWaiting to start until the intro exists. The intro is the easiest thing to shoot and the worst thing to wait on.
The rule that makes it work

A block only earns a chapter if it carries something. If it has no cargo it is B-roll, not a chapter. This is the same line that governs the cut below, and it is repeated here on purpose, because pass 2 and pass 3 are exactly where it gets decided.

Why we work this way. Seven weeks passed with zero long-form shipped, and the record shows the room was waiting on a script. Assembly-first means the cut exists before the missing pieces do, so a missing piece turns into a 90 second ask instead of a shoot day nobody schedules.

The Opener

The intro is shot last. It is still built first.

The build order says the intro is the last thing shot, and that is right: it gets shot against a cut that already exists. But the shape of it gets decided in pass 1, alongside the spine, because the first thirty seconds are where a six-minute video is won or lost and you cannot improvise them at the end. Five beats. Nothing in front of them.

1 Cold open 0:00 to 0:06
The best frame of the shoot, ambient sound only.

No music, no talking, no logo. One shot that could not have been taken anywhere else. It buys six seconds and it is the only thing in the video allowed to run on pure atmosphere.

Not thisA montage. Six seconds is one shot. If it takes three cuts to establish the place, the place was not established.
2 The hook 0:06 to 0:18
The talking head. The spine loop opens here.

The claim the whole video holds, stated flat, withholding the payoff. Run the open-versus-closed test on it before it is ever shot. Topic crystal clear in two seconds, answer withheld for six minutes.

Not thisIntroducing yourself. Nobody who just clicked cares who you are yet. They care whether the claim is true.
3 Title card 0:18 to 0:20
Two seconds, burned in.

The thumbnail's three or four words, not the full title. It confirms the viewer is in the right place and it makes the thumbnail feel like a promise that was kept.

Not thisA branded animated intro. It is the highest-drop-off two seconds available and it is worth nothing.
4 The promise 0:20 to 0:30
What they get by staying, as a question rather than a contents page.

Name the second thing they will find out, before the first one resolves. This is the retention loop from open-loop-mechanics.md doing its job at long-form scale. It is the difference between a viewer who is curious and a viewer who is committed.

Not this'In this video I'm going to show you three things.' That is a table of contents, and a table of contents closes every loop it names.
5 First payoff before 1:00
One concrete thing. A number, a name, a fact.

Something lands inside the first minute or nothing later recovers. It does not have to be the big one. It has to be real, and it has to prove the video pays what it opens.

Not thisHolding everything for the back half. A viewer who has been promised twice and paid zero times at sixty seconds is already gone.
The trap

The atmosphere open with no claim underneath it. Twenty five seconds of beautiful drone footage and a music bed feels like a real opener in the timeline and reads as a screensaver to somebody deciding whether to stay. Six seconds of that is a cold open. Twenty five is a bounce.

How you find out if it worked

The retention curve, and nothing else. `python3 scripts/youtube-analytics/pull.py --curves` returns audienceWatchRatio across elapsedVideoTimeRatio for every video on the channel, which is literally a graph of where people left. Read the first ten percent of the curve on the two videos that worked and copy the shape. Opinions about openers are free; the curve is the only thing that settles one.

Watch order versus assembly order still holds. The opener is the last thing on the timeline to be finished and one of the first to be decided. Deciding it early is not the same as cutting it early, and the build order above is about cutting.
The Title

The title is the first loop.

On YouTube the title and the thumbnail are the entire hook, because they are the only thing that exists before anybody presses play. So the open versus closed test from the Editor Room runs on the title before it runs on anything else.

The test, run it on every title

After reading the title alone, does the viewer have a complete takeaway, or an unanswered question? A complete takeaway means the title is closed. Rewrite it.

Working title, during the cut

Names the footage so everybody in the room can find it. Vibes at Sunset, Libby Hill. It is a filing label, and it is what the folder on the Drive is called. It is never what goes on YouTube.

Shipped title, written last

The headline at the top of the assignment below is the current title candidate, and it is the one that ships. The Hill That Named Richmond. It gets rewritten against the finished cut, never before it, because until the cut exists you do not know what the video actually turned out to be about.

The hook, say it exactly

Everybody who tells you Richmond is boring has never stood on this hill at eight o'clock.

Open or closed: Open. It stakes a claim the viewer has to test and withholds both the hill and what happens on it. Topic is clear in two seconds.

Effortless belonging. The feeling that this is a normal weeknight for people who live here and you are the only one who did not know about it. Not spectacle, not an event ad, not FOMO. The moment it starts performing how special it is, the viewer stops believing it. Cut it like you are already a regular and you are just showing a friend where to park.

The party cut. Crowd, drone push, sun down, music, done. It will look gorgeous, it will get a nice comment section, and it recruits exactly the wrong buyer. The same footage with the cargo attached does both jobs at once. If a chapter has no cargo, it is B-roll, not a chapter.

Hard Gate

The cargo is price, not people.

The cargo is price, inventory, and days on market. Never who lives there and never who is arriving.

WhyThe event note in the footage library describes the crowd as a live picture of who is moving into the East End. That is a fine internal observation and it is an unusable on-camera line. Church Hill is a historically Black neighborhood in fast price change, and characterizing who lives in or moves to a neighborhood is the exact pattern the audience gate drops on sight. It is also the kind of line that ends a license.
Never say
  • Any description of who lives in Church Hill or the East End, or who is moving there.
  • Any 'the neighborhood is changing' framing that means people rather than prices.
  • Safe, sketchy, up-and-coming, transitional, good schools, family-friendly. All of them.
  • Any shot held long enough to make an individual in the crowd the subject.
Say instead
  • Median price, price per square foot, days on market, active inventory, permits, what is under construction.
  • The history of the overlook and the naming of the city.
  • Wide crowd texture where no one person is the subject.
Bea and Cess have never been to Richmond and cannot be expected to feel where this line sits. That is why it is a gate on the brief and not a footnote.
