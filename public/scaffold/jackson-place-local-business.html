One · The package
The move

A competitor can hand a buyer a furniture credit. Nobody can hand them Jackson Ward.

Every Jackson Place buyer closes with The 2nd Street Welcome: roughly $5,000 in credit at the independent businesses within a five minute walk of their front door. Sunday dinner at Mama J's. Lunch at Big Herm's. A bottle from Penny's. Six of these businesses sit on N. 2nd Street, the same street your ten flagship homes face.

The merchants fund most of it in kind, because a $100 dinner credit costs a kitchen about $30, and because 41 new households arriving one block away is the cheapest customer acquisition they will ever see.

Why a price cut is the worst version of this

On a $536,000 home, five thousand dollars is nine tenths of one percent. It disappears into the closing statement, it compresses your own comps for every later phase, and it changes nobody's mind.

Option A

Cut the price $5,000

Costs you $205,000 off an $18.51M sellout. Invisible at 0.9% of price. Differentiates you from nothing, and sets a comp that follows you into every appraisal.

Option B

Buy a furniture or appliance credit

Real cash to a vendor outside the neighborhood. Every competitor can copy it by Friday. It says nothing about where the home actually is.

Option C · The move

Give them the neighborhood

The same $5,000 of felt value for roughly half the cost, and every dollar you do spend lands as revenue in a Jackson Ward register instead of vanishing off your price sheet.

Two · The question you are actually going to get asked
The objection

Somebody is going to call this gentrification. You should have the answer before they ask.

Jackson Ward has been cleared once already. When the highway went through in the 1950s it took roughly 1,000 homes and displaced more than a thousand Jackson Ward residents and business owners. Citywide the interstate program displaced around 2,200 Black families, about ten percent of Richmond's Black population.

And it did not stop. Between 2010 and 2020, Jackson Ward's Black population fell another ten percent. Wave one was the highway. Wave two was gentrification. New for sale product on RRHA land will be read as wave three unless somebody puts money back on the block.

"Jackson Place, working in close partnership with the Historic Jackson Ward Association, aims to restore and revitalize an area impacted by I 95 construction. A catalyst for the community's Place Making initiative." Emerge Construction Group, your own words

This is that sentence with a dollar sign attached. $205,000 of buyer spending routed into the independent businesses of this neighborhood, sourced from the Association's own directory, with the Black owned institutions of Jackson Ward named and paid. Not a statement. A ledger.

The frame

Revitalization, not replacement

Jackson Ward was the Harlem of the South, the address where Maggie L. Walker chartered the first bank ever led by a Black woman in America, in 1903, on these blocks. The story is not that we are bringing something. It is that we are helping something come back.

The partner

The 2nd Street Festival

Jackson Ward's flagship heritage festival, run by Venture Richmond with the City. 38th annual, October 3 and 4, 2026. Its own materials call 2nd Street the heart and soul of Richmond's African American community. Jackson Place should be on that street that weekend, as a sponsor and a neighbor.

The receipt

A number you can publish

We report the redemption ledger quarterly: exactly how many dollars landed in Jackson Ward businesses. That is a figure you can take to RRHA, to the city, and to anyone who asks whether this project gave or took.

The line we hold. The homes are marketed to everyone, on identical terms, and every buyer receives the same package. Equal Housing Opportunity, without exception. What changes is where the money goes. That is a vendor decision, and it is the honest answer to the displacement question.
