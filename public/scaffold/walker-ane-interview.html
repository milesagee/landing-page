03
Your thesis

The channel is the strategy.

This is the strongest thing you will say all morning. It is a strategic argument built entirely on federal data, and it reframes their whole distribution problem.

The three numbers

56 percent of rural hospitals use some form of predictive AI, against 81 percent of urban hospitals.

Critical access hospitals, the small rural facilities at the center of this grant, sit at 50 percent against 80 percent for everyone else.

And independent facilities are at 37 percent, while hospitals inside multi-hospital systems are at 86 percent.

Federal ASTP / ONC Health IT data brief on hospital predictive AI, 2023 to 2024, as reported by the American Hospital Association, November 2025

Now the move. One more fact turns those numbers into a strategy instead of a statistic.

The fact that changes everything

As of 2024, roughly 80 percent of hospitals using predictive AI got it through their electronic health record vendor.

Same federal data brief

Say thisTap to copy

Here is what those numbers mean for us. AI mostly reaches nurses through their employer's EHR vendor. That is the default channel. But the nurses this grant exists to serve work in exactly the places where that channel is weakest, small, independent, rural facilities that are last in line for the vendor rollout.

So if we build an awareness campaign that assumes the employer will carry the message, we will systematically miss the people the grant was funded to reach. The distribution channel is not a tactic here. It is the strategy.

Which means going direct to the nurse. State nurses associations, ANCC credential holders, nursing schools, and above all nurse-to-nurse. Not through the institution.

If you say nothing else well tomorrow, say this well. It is the difference between a candidate who would run their campaigns and a candidate who would shape their program.

04
Weak spot one, now handled

The workflows, named specifically enough that a nurse believes you.

You are not claiming clinical expertise. You are proving you did the homework on what these tools actually do at the bedside. Name real systems. Vagueness is what gets caught.

Ambient documentation

What
The tool listens to the visit and drafts the note. Abridge shipped a nursing version; Mayo Clinic is building with them. Epic has ambient documentation running inside the record itself.
Felt as
Early reports put it at roughly 15 minutes saved per admission or discharge. That is the most concrete win in the category.
Angle
This is your lead story. It gives time back rather than taking judgment away, so it is the least threatening entry point into the whole topic.
Rural
Ambient tools are often bundled with the EHR. If a facility has a thin EHR deployment, the nurse may never see it. Which is the thesis again.

Deterioration and sepsis early warning

What
Predictive models watch vitals and labs and flag a patient trending the wrong way before it is obvious.
Felt as
This is where trust is genuinely earned or lost. Too many false alarms and nurses stop looking. Alarm fatigue is a real clinical safety concept and naming it shows you are not naive.
Angle
Never message this as the computer catching what the nurse missed. Message it as a second set of eyes on a twelve hour shift. The nurse stays the decider.
Rural
Highest value where there is no rapid response team down the hall. Also where the model was least likely to be validated on that population.

Virtual nursing

What
An experienced nurse joins by camera to handle admissions, discharge teaching, and documentation while bedside staff stay hands-on. Parkview Health runs it with Care.ai. Sentara has it operational.
Felt as
The most emotionally loaded one. It looks like a staffing decision, not a technology decision, so it is where replacement fear is sharpest.
Angle
Handle carefully or not at all in early campaigns. Do not lead with it.
Rural
Bandwidth dependent. Rural broadband is a documented barrier, so a video-first program can exclude the exact audience it targets.

Acuity, staffing, and patient messaging

What
Models that predict census and acuity to build assignments, plus drafting help for patient inbox messages.
Felt as
Staffing is the third rail. Nurses have fought over ratios for decades. An algorithm touching assignments reads as management, not as help.
Angle
If they ask what you would avoid in year one, this is your answer. Knowing what not to market is a senior instinct.
Rural
Thin margins make labor-optimization tools arrive with a cost-cutting shadow attached. That shadow lands on your campaign.
The one number, and how to say it

In one emergency department time and motion study, nurses spent about 27 percent of their time in the electronic record and about 25 percent in direct patient care. More time with the chart than the patient.

Say in one emergency department study out loud. Scoping a number instead of inflating it is the credibility move, and in a room full of people who will fact-check you later, it is the safer and stronger play.

05
Weak spot two, now handled

Using AI to run the comms, with a human gate on every stage.

Everyone will say they use AI for efficiency. You are going to describe an actual operating loop, name what it automates, and name what you refuse to automate. The refusals are what make it credible.

1

Brief

Every campaign starts from a written brief: the audience segment, the belief we are trying to move, the proof we have, and the single measurable outcome. AI drafts the brief from the program materials so a campaign never starts from a blank page.

Gate: a nurse reviews the belief statement. Never marketing alone.
2

Produce

One brief becomes the full asset set at once: the email, the social variants, the landing copy, the SEO structure, the script. This is where the real time savings are, and it is what a three-year grant team with a small headcount actually needs.

Gate: no clinical claim ships without clinical review.
3

Distribute

Marketing automation carries it, segmented by practice setting and geography rather than by job title, because a nurse in a critical access hospital and a nurse in an academic center are not the same audience even at the same career stage.

Gate: every send has a rural-reachability check. Bandwidth, device, timing against shift patterns.
4

Measure

Engagement data is the raw input, not the answer. AI clusters open-ended responses, comments, and survey text into recurring objections, so we learn the shape of the resistance and not just the click rate.

Gate: a human reads the raw verbatims every cycle. Summaries hide the thing that matters.
5

Feed back

Findings write back into the next brief and, critically, back to the curriculum team. If three thousand nurses keep raising the same objection, that is not a messaging problem. That is a module the training should contain.

Gate: quarterly written synthesis to the VP of AI and Digital Health Programs.
Close on thisTap to copy

One more thing, and I think it matters more here than it would anywhere else. If we are the organization teaching nurses to use AI responsibly, our own marketing has to be able to survive that same standard.

So I would want a written policy for how this team uses AI internally, what gets disclosed, what always gets human review, and what we will never generate. Not as compliance. As proof of the thing we are asking them to believe.

If a nurse ever asks whether an email we sent was AI-written, I want the honest answer to be a good answer.

Sit still for a second after that one. It is the best thing you will say, and nobody else interviewing will say it.

06
The hard four

Answers ready before they ask.

How would you measure this? The outcome is a belief change.

Name the ladder, then say where the real measurement lives. Reach, engagement, enrollment, completion, applied use, advocacy. The first two are activity metrics that make dashboards look good. The last two are the grant outcomes.

Then the honest part: the metric that matters most is whether a nurse who finished the training reports actually using AI in practice ninety days later, and whether they would tell a colleague to take it. That takes a baseline survey and a follow-up survey, and I would want that instrument designed in month one, before a single campaign runs. You cannot measure a belief change you never baselined.

Nurses think AI is here to replace them. What do you do with that?

Do not argue with it. Concede the real part first, then narrow it. The fear is not irrational, it is pattern recognition. Nurses have watched a decade of technology arrive promising to help and then show up as more clicks, more screens, and thinner staffing. The EHR was sold as a time saver. Ask a nurse how that went.

So the answer is not reassurance, it is evidence and it is authorship. The campaign should almost never feature the organization talking. It should feature nurses who tried a tool and will say plainly what it did and did not do, including where it failed. A campaign that only shows wins will be dismissed by this audience immediately, because they know better.

And the grant word here is co-designed. That is in their own announcement. The field evidence says the deployments that work are the ones where nurses were involved early and treated as designers rather than end users. The comms should mirror the curriculum: nurses as authors, not audience.

Then close it with their own policy

ANA's board-approved position statement, The Ethical Use of Artificial Intelligence in Nursing Practice, already says it: AI does not replace good nursing care. It augments, supports, and streamlines expert clinical practice.

Say this next: I would not need to invent a position on replacement, because ANA already has one, and it is better than anything marketing would write. My job is to make sure every nurse in the program has actually heard it.

ANA Center for Ethics and Human Rights, approved by the ANA Board of Directors, December 2022

That is the strongest possible ground to stand on. You are not arguing with a nurse about whether the fear is valid. You are handing them their own professional body's answer, and positioning yourself as the person who makes sure it travels.

You are not a clinician. Why should nurses listen to you?

They should not listen to me, and I would not design a campaign where they had to. My job is not to be the voice. It is to build the system that carries their voice.

The credibility on this program has to come from nurses, from your Chief Nursing Officer, from the clinical team, and from the ANCC credential behind it. What I bring is the infrastructure: the message architecture, the segmentation, the channel strategy, the measurement spine, and the discipline to keep marketing from overclaiming on a clinical subject. On this program specifically, knowing where my authority ends is a qualification, not a gap.

What would you do in your first ninety days?

Answer it in one line and point at card eight. Thirty days listening, thirty days building the architecture, thirty days shipping one campaign that is fully measured. I would rather ship one campaign we can prove than five we can only describe.

08
If they ask for the plan

Ninety days, on one screen.

Days 1 to 30
Listen, and build the baseline
  • Talk to nurses in rural and critical access settings. Not a focus group. Actual conversations, and write down the exact words they use about AI.
  • Audit what already exists: current channels, list health, what the state nurses associations can actually carry.
  • Stand up the baseline sentiment instrument before any campaign runs.
Days 31 to 60
Build the architecture
  • One message architecture shared by the campaign and the curriculum, so a nurse hears the same thing in an email and in a module.
  • Segment by practice setting and geography, not by job title.
  • Stand up the AI production loop, with the human gates written down and agreed to.
Days 61 to 90
Ship one campaign, fully measured
  • Lead with documentation relief. It is the least threatening and most concrete benefit.
  • Nurse voices carrying it, including the honest limitations.
  • Report against enrollment and completion, not impressions. Then write the first quarterly synthesis back to the program team.
Two things not to say

Do not quote a percentage of nurses who distrust AI. I went looking for one in the American Nurses Foundation's own survey series and could not verify that a published AI figure exists. If you cite a number to the people who own the survey and you are wrong, you do not recover in that room. Ask the question in card seven instead.

Do not round the documentation number up. Say in one emergency department study, about 27 percent. The scoping is not weakness. In front of a panel that can look it up, it is the whole reason they believe the rest of what you said.
