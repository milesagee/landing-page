The gear myth

You do not need more gear to charge more.

The story in your head says a second camera, more lights, more mics come first, then the rates follow. That story is backwards, and it keeps you renting instead of earning.

Here's the proof, and Miles will vouch for it. The best video in his whole stack, the Scott's Addition piece, was shot on a DJI Osmo Pocket 3 and an iPhone. No cinema rig. The result carried because of the eye and the plan, not the price of the body.

The sequence, in the right order

Skill you already have. The FX30 and three lenses book brand work today.

Proposal is the real gap. A better pitch closes a $3,000 job on the kit you own.

Then you reinvest. A $5,000 second-camera setup that makes you $25,000 over the next two months is not a cost. It's leverage you buy with money you already earned.

Buy gear, then hope the rates come.

Renting hurts your margin because you're renting before the dubs. Land the higher-tier jobs on the kit you have, then let the surplus buy the upgrade. The gear is the reward for the reprice, not the requirement for it.

The pricing ladder

Reverse it from the life you want.

You price by feel and get negotiated down by clients who are just starting out. Flip it. Start from the monthly number you need, then work backward to the rate. Never from what a beginner can afford.

The freedom line, worked backward

Say the number that lets you drop the part-time work is $5,000 a month take-home.

As a freelancer, roughly a third goes to taxes and overhead, so you target about $7,500 gross a month.

That is three brand projects in the low thousands, or two projects plus one monthly retainer.

Not twenty party recaps at $250 that eat every weekend.

Plain EnglishNone of these words are as fancy as they sound. Package price means one flat number for the whole job, not an hourly rate you have to track. Retainer means a client pays you the same amount every month for a set amount of content, so your income is steady instead of gig to gig. Net 15 just means the client has 15 days to pay your invoice after you deliver. You are never dumb for asking what a term means. You are smart for pinning it down.

The free-work rule

Free work is a tool. Stop letting it use you.

You said you love free work when you can take your time and make something real, and you want out of the exposure collabs. You're right on both. The fix is one rule so you stop deciding case by case.

Free or discounted work is worth it only when it passes one of two gates. If a job clears a gate, take it and pour your heart in. If it clears neither, it gets a real number or a polite no.

  • Gate 1 · Passes

    A portfolio piece you direct and control.

    Like the fashion shoot. You set the vision, you own the footage, it shows the tier you want to be hired for. This is not free work. It's paying yourself in a reel that books the next three clients.

  • Gate 2 · Passes

    A written path to paid work.

    Not "maybe later." An actual named next step: a paid project on the calendar, or an intro to someone who hires. If they can't put it in writing, it isn't real yet.

  • Fails both · Retire it

    Discounted party recaps.

    Same video every time, no creative direction, no portfolio lift, no paid path. You already feel the ceiling. These are the first thing to drop as the brand work comes in.

05
The proposal

You have the skill. You're missing the close.

The creative is not the problem. The moment the price comes up is. So build a proposal that connects to their vision first, and lands the number without flinching.

You told me you ask the client their budget and go from there. That one habit is capping every job. Their budget is a ceiling. Their vision is not. When you ask "what's your budget," the best you can do is their number. When you ask "what do you want this to do for you," the number becomes yours to set.

  1. Never quote first. When someone asks your rate, ask about their vision instead. What's the outcome, who's it for, what does winning look like. Price the value, not the hours.

  2. Mirror it back until they say "that's right." Repeat their goal in your own words. When they say "exactly, that's it," you've won before the number ever comes up. That agreement is the sale.

  3. Give three options, anchored high. Good, better, best. Put the premium package at the top so the middle looks reasonable. Most people pick the middle, and your middle is now your old top.

  4. State the number, then stop talking. Say the price and go silent. Whoever speaks first loses leverage. Do not fill the pause by discounting yourself.

  5. Send a real proposal, not a text. A one-page doc with scope, deliverables, timeline, deposit, and Net 15 terms. It signals you're a studio, and it kills the negotiate-down reflex before it starts.

Plain EnglishScope is what the job includes and where it stops. Deliverables are the actual files they get (the hero film, the cutdowns, the stills). Deposit is money up front to hold the date, usually half, so a client can't ghost after you've blocked the day.

I know "don't say a number" feels impossible when they push. So here's the exact line for when someone asks your rate before you're ready. You don't dodge. You redirect, then you anchor high.

When they say "so what do you charge?"

You say"Good question. Before I throw out a number, let me make sure I price it right. What are you hoping this video actually does for you?"

If they still want a number now"For a brand project like this I usually land between [your Tier 1 floor] and [your Tier 3], depending on scope. Once I know what you need, I'll send you a real proposal with clear options."

Thenyou stop talking. A range with a real proposal behind it beats a cheap number blurted out to fill the silence.
