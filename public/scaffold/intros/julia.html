The reframe

Eight people should be writing this. Not you.

Eight subteams times about eight pages is sixty-four of your eighty-five. Your name goes on it. Your hands don't have to write all of it.

You already know this, which is why you tasked them. And they didn't do it. So the fix isn't a firmer email or a better template, because you've already sent the firm email. The ask itself has to change.

Here's the whole thesis, and it's the most useful thing on this page:

Nobody writes ten pages about themselves. Almost everybody fixes a page that's wrong about them.

So stop asking them to write. Write a rough, incomplete, partly-wrong draft of their section yourself, send it to them, and ask them to correct it. You just changed the job from three hours of composition to twenty minutes of being annoyed that you got something wrong.

Being wrong about someone's work is the most reliable way to get them to tell you about their work. Use that on purpose.

The move

The email that flips them.

Send this with the draft attached. One per subteam lead. Fill in the two blanks yourself, on your machine.

Subject line
Draft of your section, please correct by [DATE]
Body
[NAME], attached is the draft of your team's section of the history. I built it from calendar, files, and email, so it has gaps and some of it is probably wrong. I'd rather be corrected than guess. Mark it up however is easiest, track changes or just reply with notes. If I don't hear back by [DATE], this version goes in as written. Thanks, Julia

Short on purpose so you can retype it accurately off your screen. Tap it to select the whole thing if you're on a personal device.

Why each piece is doing work

  • "Some of it is probably wrong." Removes their fear of being graded and hands them the easier role, the corrector. You've pre-absorbed the blame.
  • "However is easiest." Kills the excuse about not having time to do it properly. Notes in a reply counts.
  • "This version goes in as written." The lever. Silence used to be free for them. Now silence has a result with their name on it. This is standard practice and it's fair, because you gave them the draft and the date.
  • No apology, no "sorry to bug you," no "when you get a chance." Every softener you add gives back the deadline you just created.

Give them two weeks, not three days. A tight date reads as panic and invites negotiation. A calm date reads as a schedule that already exists and they're inside it.

The retrieval

You already have two years of evidence.

It's sitting in your calendar and your files. Nothing here installs anything, plugs anything in, or automates anything.

Move one. Export your calendar. Do this first.

This is the highest-leverage hour in the whole project. Your calendar from January 2025 to December 2026 is a literal dated record of every meeting, review, offsite, and milestone your team ran. Outlook exports it to a spreadsheet natively, and the file lands on your machine and stays there.

  • In Outlook, go to File, then Open & Export, then Import/Export.
  • Choose Export to a file, then Comma Separated Values.
  • Pick your Calendar folder. It will ask for a date range. Give it 1/1/2025 through 12/31/2026.
  • Save it somewhere on your government machine and open it in Excel.
  • Add one column: Subteam. Sort by date. Tag as you scroll.

This click path is for classic Outlook desktop. If you're on new Outlook for Windows the export may sit somewhere else or be missing, and if so tell Miles and I'll find you the current path.

When you're done you have a dated, sorted, subteam-tagged skeleton of twenty-four months. Every chapter in the document hangs off that one spreadsheet. It's also the thing that would take longest to reconstruct if you never did it, which is exactly why it goes first.

Move two. Sort SharePoint by Modified date.

Every deck, report, and deliverable in your document libraries is a dated piece of proof that work happened. Sort the whole library by Modified, oldest first, and walk it. Version history tells you who was actually driving a document, not just who owns the folder.

Second sheet in the same workbook: date, subteam, artifact, what it proves. That becomes an appendix on its own, and appendices count toward your page total.

Move three. Search Outlook on a grid, not a hunch.

Eight subteams by eight quarters is sixty-four small searches. That's a task you can finish. "Find everything about the last two years" is not.

Move four. Draft from the evidence, not from memory.

With the calendar spine, the artifact ledger, and the email grid, you can write a rough eight pages for a subteam you weren't inside of. It'll have holes. That's the point. The holes are what the lead is going to fill when they redline it, and they'll fill them faster than they'd ever have written from scratch.

Every chapter runs the same six beats.

Same skeleton, eight times. That's not lazy, it's what makes the draft mechanical for you and the redline fast for them.

  • Charter and people. What this subteam exists to do, and who was on it.
  • What changed over two years. Reorgs, scope shifts, people in and out.
  • Major efforts, by quarter. Straight off your calendar spine.
  • What shipped. Straight off your artifact ledger.
  • What got in the way, and what they did about it. The only beat that needs them. Leave it thin and let them fill it.
  • What carries forward. The part leadership actually reads.

Write chapter one all the way through before you touch chapter two. Once one is done you have a shape to point at, and the other seven get faster every time. Eight parallel half-finished chapters is how this stalls out at page thirty.

The chase

Following up without a single bot.

You can't automate messaging, so don't try. Make it repeating instead, which is a different thing and completely allowed.

  • A recurring calendar appointment to yourself, every Tuesday, with your chase text sitting in the body. When it fires, you copy and send. It repeats. You didn't automate anything.
  • Quick Steps in Outlook for the follow-up you send most. One click instead of retyping. It's a built-in button, not an add-in and not an automation.
  • One pinned post in the Teams channel with the draft status and the dates. Standing and visible, so you're not chasing eight people in eight separate threads.
  • Track it in the same workbook. Subteam, draft sent, deadline, redline back. Three of eight coming back is a fact you can show someone. "They're not responding" isn't.
Worth raising

The question that could halve this.

You told me it's unclear what the document is even for, and there's no deadline. I'd be doing you a disservice not to say plainly: that combination is a risk to you, not just an annoyance. Undated work stays vague right up until it's suddenly urgent, and then the vagueness becomes your problem.

One question up the chain, asked once, in writing:

Who's the audience for this, and what decision does it support? It shapes how I scope it.

Three things can happen and all three are good for you. They tell you the real audience and you cut scope to fit it. Or they point you at a template that already exists and you stop inventing an architecture. Or they don't answer, and now you have it in writing that you asked, which is worth something later.

Your call entirely. You know your chain and I don't.
