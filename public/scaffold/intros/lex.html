That split is the whole answer. Trust takes a few hours of your effort each week. Everything after "she's interested" can run without you.

Why private-pay practices stall

This is a read on the pattern, not a study. A struggling practice usually has one of four gaps. Each one has a fix below.

  • Generic. "Anxiety, depression, trauma, couples, teens." Nobody feels like it was written for her.
  • Price hidden. The consult gets spent on the fee conversation, and the wrong people keep booking consults.
  • Slow reply. She finally works up the courage to reach out. Two days go by. She books with whoever answered first.
  • One source. A Psychology Today profile and hope.

The engine, five parts

  • Be findable where she's already searching. Google Business Profile, plus one website page per search: "EMDR therapist Richmond VA," "childhood trauma therapy for women Richmond." You do this once and it keeps working. No ad spend.
  • Let your price do the filtering. Show the fee on your Fees page and on the booking screen, before she books anything. Add one question to the consult request: "What makes now the right time?" Women who aren't ready or can't afford it filter themselves out. That's how you get the women who don't blink.
  • Automate everything after "I'm interested." Site button → SimplePractice shows your real open times → she books her own free 15-minute consult → confirmation and reminders go out on their own → after the consult, intake forms, your Good Faith Estimate, and a card on file all go out from one click. Save one warm follow-up email as a template for women who had the consult but didn't book. They reached out first, so one follow-up is fine. More than that and it starts to feel like chasing.
  • Build the referral side. It brings the best-paying clients. Every therapist with a waitlist, especially ones who don't do EMDR, is a source that keeps sending. So are OB-GYNs and integrative doctors. One coffee a week. Your unfair advantage is yoga. Teach trauma-informed workshops to other yoga teachers and clinicians. They become referral sources. Don't take your own yoga students as therapy clients: the NASW Code's dual-relationship rule makes that a real risk.
  • Paid ads come last, and small. Details below.

Turn ads on after the booking flow is live, run a fixed test budget for two months, and stop if it isn't producing consults.

Retargeting · not allowed

Google's ad policy lists "Abuse and trauma" as a sensitive category. Trauma services can't follow website visitors around the internet with ads. It would feel wrong to her anyway.

Facebook and Instagram ads · skip at launch

Meta removed health-condition targeting in 2022. Industry reports say it restricted health advertisers' conversion tracking further in 2025, but Meta hasn't confirmed that officially. Save social for free posting that builds trust, not for paid reach.

Your concierge month, rebuilt

Your instinct is right: up to 8 sessions a month for women who want to go deep. Researchers are testing twice-weekly EMDR for childhood-trauma PTSD, so the idea has real clinical backing. Two changes protect you:

  • Don't discount the hour. Present it as a twice-weekly track when it's clinically right for her, not as a bulk deal. 8 sessions at $175 is $1,400 a month.
  • Refund unused sessions, prorated. If paid-for sessions just expire, it can start to look legally like you're selling insurance. A prorated refund keeps you clean and keeps her trust.
  • Frame it as a phase, not forever. Stabilize first, then run a 6 to 8 week deep-work phase. That's a clear start and finish she can say yes to.

04 · Where AI fits

AI does the marketing. You do the healing.

There's a hard line, and it's simple. AI can write about your practice. It never sees a client.

Yes, all day

  • Drafting your website pages, directory profiles, and "what to expect" page.
  • Blog posts that answer real search questions, like "what does an EMDR session feel like."
  • Instagram captions and generic email templates with no client details.
  • You review every clinical claim before it goes live. AI gets things confidently wrong.

Never

  • A client's name, dates, diagnosis, or anything from a session in ChatGPT. The regular consumer version has no HIPAA agreement.
  • An AI chatbot as the first voice a trauma survivor meets. That first contact is you, or a form that goes straight to you.
  • Meta or Google ad tracking code on your booking or intake pages.

05 · Before the door opens

Four rules that protect the license you just earned.

Until the license issuesVirginia's title for you right now is "Supervisee in Social Work," and supervisees can't present themselves as independent practitioners. Build the whole site now, but keep it unpublished until you have your LCSW number. Call the Board of Social Work and confirm the timing.
Good Faith EstimateFederal law requires you to give every self-pay client a written cost estimate, within 1 to 3 business days of booking depending on how far out the appointment is. Post the "Right to a Good Faith Estimate" notice on your Fees page.
TestimonialsNever ask a current client for a testimonial. The NASW Code prohibits it. Your credibility on the site comes from your training, your approach, and your words.
Prepaid sessionsUnused sessions get refunded, prorated. Always.

None of this is legal advice. It's the checklist to take to the Board, and to an attorney or NASW Virginia if anything feels unclear.

06 · The runway

Eight weeks. One lane at a time.

  • Weeks 1 and 2 · Words and numbers. Lock the rate. Lock the positioning line. Draft all six pages with the prompts above, then rewrite them in your own voice.
  • Weeks 3 and 4 · The engine. Start the SimplePractice trial. Set up your services and fees, intake and consent forms, the Good Faith Estimate template, and a free 15-minute consult. No real clients in the trial.
  • Weeks 5 and 6 · Being findable. Photos. Draft your Google Business Profile, Psychology Today, TherapyDen, and Therapy Finder listings, all unpublished. List every therapist you know with a waitlist. Call the Board about website timing.
  • Weeks 7 and 8 · The dry run. Book yourself as a fake client, start to finish, on your phone. Find what breaks. Line up business setup, liability insurance, and your NPI with whoever's advising you.
  • License day. Upgrade to a paid plan and sign the HIPAA agreement. Publish the site. Turn on the listings. Text your referral list. Your door is open.
