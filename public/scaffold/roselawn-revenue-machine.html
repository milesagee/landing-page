The one idea everything else hangs on

The plot is not the product. The plot is the customer.

The position

Every plot you sell to a living person is a signed commitment to come back and spend three to five times more, at whatever your prices are on the day they need you.

"Pre-selling merchandise is a lose-lose for the cemetery. These people could live 40 to 50 more years. I'm doing funerals now where they prepaid for this stuff 40 years ago, and it cost the cemetery money. I will sell the plots, but I will not sell the opening and closing. However, I will sell plots, which means they are locked in to come to me eventually, and they're going to pay me everything else also eventually." Scott Morgan

Most operators in your industry do the opposite, and most of them do not understand why it hurts. Here is the difference in one frame.

What the industry sells
Prepaid services and merchandise
You collect 1986 dollars. You deliver the vault, the marker, the opening and closing at 2026 costs. The gap between the two is yours to eat, and you have been eating it.
Inflation is working against you.
What you sell
The plot, and only the plot
You collect today's dollars for the ground. Everything after it, the interment, the memorial, the vault, the rest of the family, gets billed at whatever your price sheet says on the day it happens.
Inflation is working for you.
Why this matters more than it sounds: your refusal to pre-sell services is not caution. It is the correct capital structure for a business that gets paid decades after it signs. Every recommendation on this page is built to protect it. Nothing here asks you to pre-sell a service.

01
The graveside is the highest intent room you will ever have
Zero cost
Every interment puts 20 to 100 relatives physically on your ground, with a permanent reason to come back. Nobody is offered the space next to it.
What is happening now A family buries someone. They leave. Some fraction of them will eventually buy somewhere, and you find out only if they call you. What to do instead
  • Ask at the memorial setting appointment, never at the funeral. The funeral is not a sales environment and treating it like one would cost you the reputation that built this business.
  • The offer is simple and it is not a pitch: the adjoining spaces in that section are available, here is what they hold, here is what they cost today.
  • Log every family member who attended. That list is the most valuable list you own and right now it does not exist on paper.
Why it is first It costs nothing, it requires no new staff, and it converts at rates no advertising channel can approach, because the emotional decision has already been made. Every additional space sold to a family that already buried someone there is nearly pure margin on ground you have already cleared.

The licensing move that matters most The Virginia crematory registration application makes you elect a business model. One option is "crematory not owned by a funeral establishment, offering cremation to funeral establishments only." Electing that keeps you out of funeral service establishment licensure entirely, and it means you are never competing with the funeral directors who would be your customers. The columbarium argument

A granite niche costs roughly $460 to $650 to build and sells in a $2,200 to $4,250 band, on a footprint of a few square feet instead of twenty-seven.

Put that next to a grave. Mount Olivet, the only cemetery in this market that publishes real prices, sells a 3ft by 9ft casket grave for $1,600, which is about $59 per square foot, and a 3ft by 3ft ash grave for $800, about $89 per square foot. A columbarium niche beats both by a wide margin on the same ground you already own, already cleared, and already mow. That per-square-foot comparison is arithmetic on the sourced prices, not a published industry statistic. No ICCFA or CANA revenue-per-square-foot figure was locatable.
What the wholesale rate is not: two Richmond price lists show a crematory charge of $425 (Morrissett, 2022) and $650 (Cremation Society of Virginia, 2026). Those are pass-through retail line items, almost certainly above the real trade rate. The national benchmark for what a funeral home pays a third-party crematory is $150 to $350 per case. Nobody publishes trade pricing, because the FTC Funeral Rule does not require it. Before you set a price, call two of those 14 houses and ask what they pay now. That one conversation is worth more than any further research.
Your unfair advantage on the build: the published capex ranges assume you are hiring out site work, pad prep, and grading. You have 17 pieces of equipment and a crew. That line comes off your budget and stays on everyone else's, which is exactly why a project that prices at $270K nationally should price lower for you.
The twelve acres

Wetlands are the one exception to everything above

Every other acre you own is worth more platted than sold. Wetlands cannot be platted, which means they are the only ground on the property where selling is genuinely on the table. There are three paths and they are not equal.

Path 01 · Do not do this
Sponsor your own mitigation bank
I expected this to be the clever answer. It is not, and the reason is specific: land that is already wetland cannot be restored into credits. Preservation gets discounted hard, at 10 acres to 1 credit in the most recent Norfolk District bank. Your 12 acres yields about 1.2 credits, worth roughly $108,000 at the James basin reference price of $90,000. Sponsoring the bank costs $80,000 to $1.2M plus construction, plus perpetual maintenance security, and averages over three years to approval. The cost floor is above the revenue ceiling.
Path 02 · The likely answer
Donated conservation easement
Virginia's Land Preservation Tax Credit is 40% of the appraised value of the donation, and the credits are transferable, so you can sell them even if you cannot use them. The secondary market nets donors roughly 80 to 85 cents on the dollar after a 5% transfer fee. You are donating development rights on ground you can never plat anyway. On an illustrative $150K to $420K easement value, that is roughly $46,000 to $136,000 in cash, in the year of conveyance rather than in a decade.
Path 03 · The floor
Sell it, or simply keep it
Hanover assesses raw A-1 acreage on this corridor around $7,600 per acre, and non-developable wetland sits below that, so an arm's length sale is plausibly tens of thousands. The exception is a neighboring project that needs it for stormwater, buffer, or Chesapeake Bay Act compliance, where the price is set by their need instead of the land. And doing nothing is cheap: the 10-acre parcel at your address carries $723.74 a year in tax. Wetland buffer costs almost nothing to hold.
The one thing to spend money on first, and it is small: order a wetland delineation and an Army Corps jurisdictional determination. Every path above depends on how many acres are truly jurisdictional, and on one specific question. In March 2025 Virginia DEQ and the Corps clarified that actively managed loblolly pine plantation is now eligible as a restoration site rather than being stuck at preservation ratios. If any part of those 12 acres is pine plantation or ditched or prior-converted ground rather than mature forested wetland, the credit math changes materially in your favor. Nobody can tell you that without walking it.
Then make two phone calls, not a project. One to an active James basin bank sponsor about optioning the acreage as preservation or buffer inventory on top of their restoration core, which moves all the cost and all the years onto them. One to a Virginia easement holder about whether they would accept a wetland-only easement. Whichever comes back with a real number wins. If neither produces something you like inside 90 days, keep it as cemetery buffer and stop thinking about it. Against $60,000 that turns two acres into two thousand plots, every wetland path here is a rounding error on your attention.
What the county record does and does not show. Hanover's floodplain layer returns no records for either cemetery parcel. That is not the same as a wetlands finding, because jurisdictional wetlands are delineated by DEQ and the Army Corps rather than mapped by the county, so it neither confirms nor rules out the 12 acres. The thing that would settle it is a jurisdictional determination letter or the sheet set from your approved site plan. If you have either one, that closes the question immediately.
Two record discrepancies worth a minute of your time. Your website says 75 acres with 45 cleared, while Hanover's tax roll shows 41.386 acres across the two parcels titled to Greenlawn Memory Gardens, Inc. The gap is roughly the 20 acres that moved to Mountain Rd Properties LLC at deed book 3377. Separately, no public record anywhere states the 4,000 plot count, so that figure should be tied to your approved plat or your own interment map before either of us repeats it. Neither is a problem. Both are just worth knowing before a number gets quoted to someone.
